Government employees: how to protect sensitive official documents on your computer

Government offices handle files that matter to many people: draft policies, freedom-of-information requests, tender documents, personnel records, complaints and applications with citizens' details. Many of these sit on an ordinary office desktop or a laptop. This guide explains how to protect sensitive official files, and, just as important, when a third-party tool is not the right answer.

Short answer: classified information (whatever your government calls its levels, such as Secret or Top Secret) must be handled only as your department's security instructions allow, usually on approved and often stand-alone systems. Don't put it in any third-party app, including Clavis Encrypt, unless your department has approved that. For unclassified but sensitive files, such as drafts, personnel data, tender comparisons and citizens' requests, encrypting them on your work computer protects them if the machine is lost, shared or sent for repair. Clavis Encrypt does this offline, with AES-256-GCM, and needs no account or internet connection.

First, check what your department requires

  • Classified material follows your government's classification rules, your department's security instructions and the advice of your IT and security officers. Those rules come first, always.
  • Your organisation's information-security policy usually says how sensitive data on laptops, desktops and removable media must be protected, and which tools are allowed. Standards bodies such as the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) describe full-disk, volume and file/folder encryption as ways to stop unauthorised people reading data stored on end-user devices.
  • Official email: use your official account for official communication, as your department's policy requires, not personal email.
  • Software: install only what your department permits. If you want to use a file-encryption tool, ask your IT team or information-security officer first. This page explains what they will want to know.

Where encryption helps (unclassified but sensitive files)

  • Draft documents before they are final or public.
  • Personnel data: staff records, appraisal notes, salary and leave statements.
  • Citizens' data: information requests, complaints and petitions, beneficiary lists with ID or phone numbers.
  • Procurement: tender comparisons and bid evaluations before award.
  • Shared office PCs used by several staff, and computers sent out for repair.

How to protect them (once your IT team agrees)

  1. Install Clavis Encrypt. It installs per user, without administrator rights, and works fully offline. It goes online only to check for signed updates, and that check can be switched off in Settings.
  2. Create a vault with a strong password, and keep the one-time recovery key as your office requires, for example sealed and held by your manager or security officer.
  3. Encrypt sensitive files: right-click → Clavis → Encrypt. File names are hidden too.
  4. Use separate vaults (Pro) for different teams or subjects, each with its own password.
  5. Back up the encrypted files to the storage your office approves. They stay encrypted there.

Questions

How can a government employee protect sensitive official files on a computer?

Follow your department's security instructions first; classified material must stay on approved systems. For unclassified but sensitive files, such as drafts, personnel data and citizens' requests, encrypt them on your work computer with a tool your IT team approves. Clavis Encrypt works offline with AES-256-GCM and needs no account.

Can I use a third-party encryption app for classified documents?

Not unless your department has approved it. Classified information must be handled as your government's and department's security instructions require, typically on approved, often stand-alone systems. Clavis Encrypt is not certified for classified data.

Do I need permission to install encryption software on a government computer?

Ask first. Many departments allow only approved software on official computers, even when a program installs without administrator rights. Tell your IT team what the tool does, whether it sends data anywhere and how files are recovered if a password is lost.

Does Clavis Encrypt need internet or administrator rights?

No. It installs per user without administrator rights and works offline. It goes online only to check for signed updates, and that can be turned off.
