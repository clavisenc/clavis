Small businesses: how to protect employee, salary and customer data on office PCs

In a small business, the "HR system" is often an Excel sheet on the owner's laptop, and the customer database is a folder of invoices and a phone-number list. Salaries, ID copies, tax numbers, bank details and customer addresses all sit on PCs that several people use. Here's a practical way to protect that data without enterprise software.

Short answer: keep payroll sheets, employee ID copies and customer lists encrypted on your office PCs, give each person their own Windows login, and back up the encrypted files. A file-encryption app such as Clavis Encrypt does this offline with AES-256-GCM, for free for individual files or a one-time $19 for folders and automatic encryption. Encryption, access control and backups are the kind of "reasonable security" that data-protection laws such as the EU's GDPR expect.

Which data needs protecting

  • Payroll: salary sheets, payslips, bank account numbers, tax and pension details.
  • Employee records: ID copies (passport, national ID card, tax ID), contracts and offer letters, appraisal notes, medical certificates.
  • Customers: contact lists, addresses, invoices, tax registration details, payment records.
  • Business secrets: pricing, supplier rates, accounts.

Why it matters

  • Data-protection laws: many countries expect any business that holds customers' or employees' personal data to keep it reasonably secure. The EU's GDPR (Article 32) requires security appropriate to the risk and lists "the pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data" as an example. California's CCPA lets consumers sue a covered business when their nonencrypted and nonredacted personal information is stolen because the business didn't maintain reasonable security. India's DPDP Rules, 2025 (Rule 6) name encryption, access control, logs and backups among the safeguards.
  • Trust: employees and customers expect their salary, ID and payment details to stay private, and a leak is hard to undo.
  • Everyday risks: a staff member leaving with a USB stick, a stolen laptop, a shared reception PC, ransomware.

Set it up in an afternoon

  1. Give everyone their own Windows account on shared PCs, and keep admin rights to the owner.
  2. Install Clavis Encrypt on the PCs that hold sensitive files (no administrator rights needed).
  3. Create a vault for HR and payroll that only the owner or HR person knows the password to, and keep the recovery key in a safe. With Clavis Pro, add separate vaults for accounts and sales.
  4. Encrypt the payroll workbook, employee folders and customer lists. Pro encrypts whole folders and can watch a folder so new files are encrypted automatically. Employee ID copies and payslips that arrived by email or a messaging app often sit forgotten in Downloads; Clavis Pro's Find sensitive files helps you find ID, bank, salary and tax documents on the PC: see where forgotten ID and bank scans hide.
  5. Edit the salary sheet with Pro's Open: it opens in Excel and is re-encrypted when you close it.
  6. Back up encrypted files weekly to an external drive kept off-site, or to the cloud with Premium's encrypt-to-cloud-folder.
  7. If several people need the same files, such as the owner and an accountant, keep them in a Clavis team folder in OneDrive, Google Drive, Dropbox or on the office network drive: each person opens it with their own Clavis ID, so there's no shared password, and removing someone locks them out of new and changed files (creating a team folder is part of Premium).
  8. When someone leaves, change the passwords of the vaults they knew, and remove them from any team folder and from the cloud folder itself.

What Clavis can and can't do here

  • It can encrypt payroll, HR and customer files on each PC, keep copies encrypted on USB sticks, in email and in the cloud, and help you find forgotten ID, bank, salary and tax documents.
  • It can't make a business compliant on its own: data-protection laws also expect policies, access control, staff training and a plan for breaches. Find sensitive files helps you find documents but can miss some, and for many countries' ID cards and tax forms it relies on the file name.

Questions

How can a small business protect employee salary data?

Keep payroll files encrypted on the PC, restrict the vault password to the owner or HR, give staff separate Windows logins, and keep encrypted backups. Clavis Encrypt encrypts files offline with AES-256-GCM; Pro adds folders and automatic encryption for a one-time $19.

Do data-protection laws apply to small businesses?

Often, yes. The EU's GDPR applies to businesses of any size that handle personal data and requires security appropriate to the risk, with encryption given as an example. Other laws, such as California's CCPA, cover only businesses that meet certain thresholds. Check the rules where you operate, or ask a lawyer.

Where should a small business keep copies of employee ID documents?

In one encrypted folder that only the owner or HR can open, not in email, chat apps or Downloads. Keep copies only as long as you need them, and delete the rest.

Is an Excel password enough to protect a salary sheet?

Not on its own. Excel's file password is better than nothing, but sheet-protection passwords are easily removed and the file name still reveals what it is. Encrypting the whole file hides its name and contents.

What should I do when an employee who knew the password leaves?

Change the vault password and, ideally, re-encrypt the files with the new one. Clavis lets you change a vault password in Settings.
