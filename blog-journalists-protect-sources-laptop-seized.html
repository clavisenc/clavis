Journalists: how to protect sources and files if your laptop is seized

If you report on sensitive stories, your laptop holds more than your work. It holds documents, photos, recordings and the names of people who trusted you. This guide shows how to encrypt that material so a lost, stolen or seized device doesn't expose them, and is honest about what encryption can't do.

Short answer: keep source material in encrypted files, not in ordinary folders. With a file-encryption app such as Clavis Encrypt (free, works offline on Windows and Linux), each file is sealed with AES-256-GCM and can only be opened with your password. Anyone who copies the drive, including from a laptop that leaves your hands, gets unreadable files with random names. Turn on full-disk encryption (BitLocker or Device encryption) as well, and lock your files whenever you're not using them. Encryption is a technical protection; whether you can be required to unlock a device depends on where you are, so get legal advice if that's a risk for you.

Why ordinary passwords aren't enough

  • A Windows login password doesn't encrypt your files. Anyone who removes the drive, or boots the laptop from a USB stick, can read everything on it. The EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense guide makes the same point: locking a device with a password or PIN may not protect your data if the device itself is seized.
  • Cloud folders sync copies elsewhere. Files in OneDrive, Google Drive or Dropbox also live on those companies' servers, outside your control.
  • Devices leave your hands more often than you think: repairs, travel, theft, a border check, an office search, or a seizure during an investigation.

How to encrypt your source material (step by step)

  1. Download Clavis Encrypt and install it. It needs no administrator rights, no account and no internet connection to work.
  2. Create a vault with a long, unique password (a sentence of five or more random words is strong and memorable). Clavis shows how long it would take to crack and warns you about weak ones.
  3. Save the one-time recovery key it shows you, and keep it away from the laptop (printed, in a safe place). It's the only way back in if you forget the password.
  4. Encrypt your sensitive files: right-click in File Explorer → Clavis → Encrypt, or drag them onto the Clavis window. Each becomes a .enc file with a random name; the real name is hidden inside.
  5. For a whole investigation folder, Clavis Pro encrypts folders in one go, and a watch folder encrypts every new file you save there automatically, so nothing is left open by mistake.
  6. When you finish working, lock Clavis. It also locks itself when the PC sleeps or Windows locks.

Habits that matter as much as the tool

  • Encrypt early. Encrypt files as soon as you receive them. On SSDs, deleted plain copies can linger in places no app can reliably wipe, so full-disk encryption is your safety net for those.
  • Turn on full-disk encryption too. BitLocker (Windows Pro) or Device encryption (many Windows Home laptops) protects the whole drive when the laptop is off. See BitLocker vs Device encryption.
  • Shut down, don't just close the lid, before travel or when you expect a search. A running, unlocked laptop is far easier to read than one that's switched off.
  • Travel light. Before crossing a border, consider carrying only the files you need for the trip. The Committee to Protect Journalists notes that if border officials take a device out of your sight, they have an opportunity to search it.
  • Separate work. Use a separate vault (Clavis Pro) for each sensitive story, each with its own password.
  • Send safely. To share a file with an editor, encrypt it for them with their Clavis ID instead of sending a password over chat. See sharing encrypted files without a password.
  • Keep encrypted backups on a second drive, so a seized laptop doesn't also mean lost work.

Laptop taken while unlocked and in useOnly files that are still encrypted. Lock Clavis and shut down when you can.

Searches, seizures and passwords: the legal side

The rules on when authorities can search or seize a device, and whether you can be made to unlock it or reveal a password, differ from country to country. They can also differ between a criminal investigation and a border crossing, and in some places the courts haven't settled them. Press-freedom groups such as the Committee to Protect Journalists and the Freedom of the Press Foundation publish digital-safety guidance for reporters who may face device seizures.

What this means for you: encryption reliably protects your files from everyone who doesn't have your password: thieves, repair shops, careless handling, and leaks of seized data. It does not decide your legal position. If your devices are seized, or you're asked to unlock them, speak to a lawyer.

Questions

How can a journalist protect sources if their laptop is seized?

Keep source material encrypted, not in ordinary folders. A file-encryption app such as Clavis Encrypt seals each file with AES-256-GCM so it can only be opened with your password; anyone who copies the drive gets unreadable files. Add full-disk encryption, shut the laptop down when it isn't in use, and keep encrypted backups elsewhere.

Can the police or border officials force me to give them my password?

It depends on the country and the situation, and in some places the law is unsettled. Encryption keeps your files unreadable to anyone without your password, but it doesn't decide your legal position. If a device is seized or you're asked to unlock it, get advice from a lawyer, and see the digital-safety guidance from groups such as CPJ and the Freedom of the Press Foundation.

Should I encrypt my laptop before crossing a border as a journalist?

Yes. Turn on full-disk encryption, keep sensitive files encrypted, shut the laptop down before the crossing, and consider carrying only the files you need. CPJ notes that a device taken out of your sight can be searched. Encryption protects the files technically; the rules on unlocking devices at borders depend on the country.

Is a Windows password enough to protect my files?

No. A Windows login password doesn't encrypt files. Anyone who removes the drive or boots from a USB stick can read them. Use file encryption, full-disk encryption, or both.

Does Clavis Encrypt upload my files anywhere?

No. Everything is encrypted on your own computer. There's no account and no cloud; the app goes online only to download signed updates.

What if I forget my password?

Use the one-time recovery key Clavis showed you when you created your vault. Keep it printed and away from the laptop. Without the password or the recovery key, no one, including the developer, can open your files.
